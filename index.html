Virtual ESL Journey to Oxford

Welcome to my travel blog! Here, I'm taking you along on my trip to England. In this section, you will learn all about my experiences, language exercises, and the progress I'm making during this journey. You can follow me on my language journey to Oxford.

We invite you to a virtual language journey to England. You will travel with other adult students who are learning English as a second language (ESL). The program takes place at a language college in Oxford. The students will live in B+B homes with friendly English speaking hosts. The college is Universal Worldwide Education, for English as a Second Language in Oxford. In weekly lessons, we will improve our English speaking skills from level A2 to B2.

The days will be packed with different activities. In the mornings, you'll have English lessons at the school. The afternoons are free for you to explore Oxford and other parts of England. In the evenings, you can relax with friends at pubs or enjoy cultural events. The main teaching method focuses on learning language patterns using complete sentences. By repeating and practicing these patterns regularly, you will improve your English speaking skills.

In this blog, you can read and hear the weekly reports from the lessons and the everyday activities. You'll have the opportunity to do exercises, complete worksheets and write your own letters or reports. Speaking English fluently is the most important goal of teaching. This trip offers a great way to learn English while experiencing English culture first-hand.

01 Udo arrives in Oxford

The scent of freshly baked bread, warm and yeasty, hangs in the crisp Oxford air. It mingles with the earthy aroma of damp leaves as you pull your worn leather suitcase behind you. The cobblestone street, lined with houses painted in cheerful pastel shades, feels a world away from the familiar concrete of home.

You pause before number fifteen, the brass number gleaming softly in the afternoon light. This is it: your B&B for the next month. A nervous flutter dances in your stomach, a mix of anticipation and the quiet fear of the unknown. You straighten your jacket, a familiar, comforting motion.

Taking a deep breath, you ascend the three stone steps to the front door. The wood is painted a glossy, inviting blue. You reach for the doorbell, a small, antique-looking contraption, and give it a firm press. A melodic chime echoes from within.

You can hear movement inside, the soft pad of footsteps approaching. The door swings open, revealing a woman with a warm smile and kind eyes. "Udo, welcome! I'm Mrs. Davies. We've been expecting you."

Her voice is gentle, with a lilting accent that's both foreign and strangely comforting. "Come in, come in," she says, gesturing you inside. "You must be tired from your journey."

The hallway is cozy and inviting, a riot of floral wallpaper and antique furniture. A grandfather clock ticks rhythmically in the corner, marking the passage of time with steady, unwavering precision. The air is thick with the scent of potpourri and something else… something savory and comforting.

"Let me take your suitcase," Mrs. Davies offers, her smile unwavering. "Your room is just upstairs. It's number three."

You relinquish your suitcase, the weight lifting from your arm. "Thank you, Mrs. Davies. It's a pleasure to be here."

"The pleasure is all ours, dear," she says, her eyes twinkling. "Now, go on up and get settled. Tea will be ready in about an hour. It's a good way to know the other students."

02 Welcome to the B&B

You mount the narrow staircase, the wooden steps creaking softly under your weight. The scent of lavender grows stronger as you ascend. At the top of the stairs, you find a small landing with three doors. Number three is clearly marked with a small brass plate. You reach for the handle, a sense of quiet anticipation building within you.

As you step into the room, you notice a small, handwritten note lying on the neatly made bed. It's addressed to you. The note is written in elegant cursive. You read: "Welcome, Udo! We're so glad to have you. Tea will be served in the main dining room at 4 PM. Looking forward to meeting you! - Mrs. Davies".

A smile spreads across your face. Tea at four. A perfect English welcome. You set the note aside and begin unpacking. Your clothes, neatly folded, find their place in the small wardrobe. Toiletries are arranged on the shelf in the even smaller en-suite bathroom. Soon, your suitcase is empty, tucked away under the bed.

With time to spare, you decide to freshen up. The water in the shower is hot and plentiful, washing away the weariness of your journey. Refreshed, you dress in your best casual attire. You want to make a good first impression.

As you descend the stairs, you hear the murmur of voices from the dining room. It seems you are not the first to arrive. Taking a deep breath, you step into the room. The dining room is bright and cheerful, bathed in the warm glow of the late afternoon sun. A long, polished table dominates the room, laden with an array of cakes, sandwiches, and teapots. Several people are already seated, chatting animatedly.

03 Smalltalk in the evening

Mrs. Davies bustles about, pouring tea and offering plates of food. "Ah, Udo, you're here! Come, come, let me introduce you." She leads you to the table. "Everyone, this is Udo, our new student from Germany. Udo, this is Maria from Spain, Kenji from Japan, and Aaliyah from Saudi Arabia."

Maria, a young woman with bright eyes and a warm smile, greets you with enthusiasm. "Welcome to Oxford, Udo! I hope you enjoy your time here." Kenji, a quiet and polite young man, nods his head in greeting. "It's nice to meet you." Aaliyah, a confident and articulate woman, extends her hand. "Welcome! I am sure you will fit in quickly."

"Please, everyone, make Udo feel at home," Mrs. Davies says, beaming. "Now, help yourselves to tea and sandwiches. And don't be shy, practice your English!" You take a seat at the table, feeling a surge of anticipation. "Thank you. It's a pleasure to meet you all." You pour yourself a cup of tea, the fragrant steam rising to your face.

"So, Udo, what brings you to Oxford?" Maria asks, her eyes sparkling with curiosity. Before you can answer, a booming voice fills the room. "Sorry I'm late! Traffic was a nightmare."

A tall man with a neatly trimmed beard strides into the dining room. "Mrs. Davies, my apologies. Ah, we have a new face! Welcome to Universal Worldwide Education." Mrs. Davies turns to you with a smile: "Udo, this is Tom, head of the teaching staff."

04 In the evening we meet new students

You rise slightly from your chair to greet Tom, extending your hand with a firm shake. "Hello, Tom. I'm Udo, from Germany. It's great to meet you. I'm a civil engineer, and I've come to Oxford to brush up on my English. I reached B2 level years ago through videos and books, but now I want to make it fluent again for work and new adventures."

Tom claps you on the shoulder, his beard twitching with a grin. "Excellent, Udo! A engineer with a global mindset—perfect for our class. We'll have you debating bridges in perfect idiom by week's end." He pulls up a chair beside Mrs. Davies, who refills his teacup with practiced grace.

Maria leans forward, her enthusiasm undimmed. "That's wonderful, Udo. As for me, I'm Maria from Spain. I run a small tapas bar in Barcelona, but I need better English to expand online and attract tourists from everywhere. I've been practicing with podcasts and Netflix, but conversations still trip me up—like saying 'delicious' when I mean 'disgusting'!"

Laughter ripples around the table, light and easy. Kenji, sipping his tea methodically, sets his cup down with a soft clink. "I am Kenji from Tokyo. I design video games, and English is essential for international teams. I study every day with apps and manga translations, but idioms confuse me. Why do you 'kick the bucket' to die? It sounds painful."

Aaliyah chuckles, her voice carrying a melodic lilt. "Kenji, that's nothing. I'm Aaliyah from Riyadh, studying business to launch my own fashion line. My English comes from American movies and YouTube, but I struggle with phrasal verbs. 'Turn up'—does it mean arrive or increase volume? I want to negotiate with suppliers worldwide, so this course is my ticket."

Mrs. Davies beams as she passes a plate of cucumber sandwiches. "Such inspiring stories! And Tom, you've heard mine before, but for Udo—I've hosted students for decades, practicing my listening while they butcher idioms."

Tom nods, biting into a scone with relish. "Spot on. My own journey started in Asia, teaching rebels how to swear properly in English. Now, I live for moments like this—turning stumbles into strides."

The conversation flows like the tea, warm and steaming, punctuated by shared mishaps: Maria's epic fail ordering "fish and chips" as "fishes and ships," Kenji's bafflement at "it's raining cats and dogs," Aaliyah's regal insistence that "queue" must be a royal command. You contribute a tale of once calling a "flat tire" a "pancake wheel," drawing fresh peals of laughter. The sun dips lower, casting golden hues across the polished wood, as plates empty and voices soften.

Finally, Tom glances at the clock. "Right, folks, tomorrow's our first lesson at nine sharp. Dream in English tonight." Mrs. Davies begins clearing cups, her gentle voice bidding goodnights. One by one, the group rises, exchanging nods and smiles laced with that shared spark of anticipation. Kenji lingers a moment, adjusting his bag, while Maria waves from the doorway.

05 The first lesson - teacher Peggy introduces herself

The next morning, sunlight filters through the tall windows of the school classroom, casting playful shadows across rows of wooden desks arranged in a loose semicircle. You settle into a seat near the front, notebook open and pen at the ready, the faint aroma of chalk dust mingling with fresh coffee from a side table. Maria slides in beside you with a quick wave, her energy undimmed, while Kenji takes a spot across the aisle, meticulously aligning his supplies. Aaliyah arrives last, flashing a bright smile as she claims the end seat. Tom pokes his head in briefly, gives a thumbs-up, and vanishes down the hall.

Peggy sweeps into the room right on time, a whirlwind of colourful scarves and sharp eyes, her heels clicking against the floorboards. She perches on the edge of the teacher's desk, clipboard in hand, and launches straight into the lesson with a dramatic flourish. "Good morning, everyone! Today, we're diving into the past—because to speak English fluently, you have to wrestle with what came before. Let me introduce myself properly, using the tenses we'll master: simple past, present perfect, present perfect continuous, and past perfect. Picture this: I worked as a journalist for fifteen years, chasing stories across continents."

She pauses, letting the words hang, her voice rising with theatrical flair. The class leans in, hooked. "I had travelled to war zones before I ever set foot in a classroom like this - dodging deadlines in dusty newsrooms from Baghdad to Buenos Aires. That's past perfect for you: something completed before another past action. But here's the twist - I've written over two hundred articles that still echo online, blending hard news with quirky human tales. Present perfect, capturing experiences that shape me now."

08-1 The pattern learning teaching method

The classroom settles into a comfortable hum as Peggy gestures toward the door, where Tom appears with a stack of worksheets. He nods a greeting, his neatly trimmed beard catching the morning light, and moves to the side table.

"Before you dive into sharing adventures," Peggy says, her voice taking on a thoughtful edge, "let me introduce you to the backbone of how we'll work here. Tom and I call it pattern learning."

Tom sets the papers down and turns to face the class, hands resting on his hips. "It sounds technical," he says, his voice warm and steady, "but really it's about something very old and very proven. Practice makes perfect."

Peggy picks up a worksheet and holds it aloft. "With these, we focus on pattern sentences. You hear a phrase, you repeat it. Again and again. Not because we want to bore you, but because language lives in repetition. The grammar, the rhythm, the pronunciation—they all become automatic when you drill the patterns that appear most often in everyday English."

Maria leans forward, her brow furrowing slightly. "Like learning a dance step?"

"Exactly like that," Tom says, nodding. "At first you think about every movement. But after enough repetition, your body knows the steps before your mind catches up. That's what we're building here. Automatization of the most important grammatical patterns."

You glance down at your notebook, the concept settling into place. It makes sense—the hours you'd spent listening to audiobooks and repeating phrases during your evening classes back home. That repetition had built something, even if you hadn't named it at the time.
